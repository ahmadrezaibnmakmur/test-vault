## 2. Building a Productive and High-Performance Culture

### Question

**What strategies do you implement to foster a productive, effective, and high-performance culture?**

### Recommended answer

I build a high-performance culture around five practices: **clarity, ownership, visibility, coaching, and continuous improvement**.

First, I translate business priorities into clear outcomes, measurable targets, and explicit ownership. This prevents a team from being busy without producing meaningful results. Second, I create a consistent operating cadence through short progress reviews, prioritization sessions, risk escalation, and stakeholder check-ins. Third, I make performance visible through project pipelines, KPI dashboards, service-level measures, and clear status definitions so that decisions are based on facts rather than assumptions.

I also give team members ownership of problems, not only tasks. I coach them to understand the process, identify root causes, recommend options, and communicate their decisions. This builds analytical judgment and reduces dependency on the manager. At the same time, I encourage people to raise risks and mistakes early, because early visibility allows the team to solve problems before they affect customers.

Finally, I use a continuous-improvement mindset: we review completed work, capture lessons learned, standardize what works, and remove activities that do not add value. This approach helped my lean team manage 118 improvement initiatives while keeping attention on the projects with the highest strategic and operational impact.

### Short spoken version

I focus on clarity, ownership, visibility, coaching, and continuous improvement. Every priority needs a measurable outcome, a clear owner, and a review cadence. I give team members ownership of problems rather than only assigning tasks, and I encourage early escalation without blame. We then turn lessons learned into better standards and repeatable ways of working.

## 3. Operational Process or SOP Framework Built from Scratch

### Question

**Could you share an example of an operational process or SOP framework you designed and implemented from scratch?**

### Recommended answer

One example was the **automation and process-improvement framework for client fund disbursement** at PT Esensi Solusi Buana. The original workflow was semi-automated and still depended heavily on manual checking, reconciliation, data adjustments, and coordination among Finance, Data, Operations, and System teams. This created delays, rework, and inconsistent execution.

I designed the framework from the ground up using Lean Six Sigma and DMAIC principles. I began with an end-to-end as-is process map and baseline KPIs, then identified waste, failure points, and root causes. From that analysis, I developed a roadmap of **22 improvement initiatives**. The operating framework included standardized master data, clear process steps and decision points, role and access controls, ownership across functions, exception handling, escalation paths, SOP documentation, and a control plan supported by ongoing performance monitoring.

Implementation was staged so that we could test controls, validate results, and manage adoption before scaling. The improvement reduced the average lead time from **15.73 hours to 14.00 hours**. The proportion of days when processing finished after 5:00 p.m. fell from **24.6% to 0%**, and the adjustment rate declined from **0.32% to 0.06%**, an approximately **82% relative reduction**. At the broader service level, the process moved from **D+3 to D+1** processing.

This project demonstrates how I approach SOP design: I do not treat an SOP as a static document. I connect the documented process with ownership, controls, system rules, exception management, KPIs, and a continuous-improvement mechanism.

### Framework summary

1. Define the service objective, scope, customer, and baseline KPIs.
2. Map the as-is process, handoffs, systems, and pain points.
3. Analyze waste, risks, failure modes, and root causes.
4. Design the to-be workflow, roles, controls, and exception paths.
5. Document the SOP, decision rules, access rights, and escalation matrix.
6. Pilot the workflow and validate operational and control results.
7. Train users, manage adoption, and deploy in stages.
8. Monitor KPIs through a control plan and improve the standard when needed.

### Short spoken version

I designed a disbursement operating framework from scratch for a process that relied on manual checking, reconciliation, and cross-functional coordination. I mapped the full workflow, established baseline KPIs, analyzed root causes, and created a 22-initiative DMAIC roadmap covering SOPs, ownership, master data, system access, exception handling, and performance controls. The work moved processing from D+3 to D+1, eliminated processing after 5 p.m., and reduced the adjustment rate by about 82%.

## 4. Operational Bottleneck, Solution, and Measurable Impact

### Question

**What is a specific operational bottleneck you proactively identified in a previous role? What solution did you deploy, and what were the measurable impacts on service delivery or cost-efficiency?**

### Recommended answer

A specific bottleneck I identified was the growing dependency on manual work in the invoice process. As transaction volume increased, manual preparation and checking limited scalability, created avoidable workload, and made service performance too dependent on additional staffing.

I mapped the invoice workflow, identified repetitive and rule-based activities, standardized the required data and validation logic, and implemented automation for the high-volume parts of the process. I retained exception handling for cases that required human judgment and introduced operational monitoring so the team could focus on discrepancies rather than repeatedly processing standard transactions.

The solution automated **94% of the previously manual invoice process** and enabled the Finance team to process more than **4,300 invoices per month**. Invoice productivity improved from **136 to 223 invoices per staff member**, an increase of approximately **64%**. The main business value was not only faster processing; it also created a more scalable operating model in which volume could grow without a proportional increase in manual workload.

### Short spoken version

I identified manual invoice processing as a scalability bottleneck. I standardized the data and rules, automated repeatable work, kept human review for exceptions, and introduced monitoring. This automated 94% of the manual process, supported more than 4,300 invoices per month, and increased invoices processed per staff member from 136 to 223—about a 64% productivity improvement.
